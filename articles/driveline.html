Data-Driven Pitching Training: What Youth Coaches Should Know

4 min read

Parents hear about pitching labs, radar guns, and weighted balls earlier every year. One of the best-known names is Driveline Baseball, a private training company in Kent, Washington, that works mostly with high school, college, and professional pitchers. Many other facilities use similar methods.

This page is a plain summary of what is known about this style of training and what it means for kids roughly 8 to 14. It is not a training guide. We do not publish throwing programs or pitching mechanics.

What “data-driven” pitching training is

These programs measure things coaches used to judge by eye: velocity, spin rate, and body movement captured with high-speed cameras and sensors. Training is then adjusted based on the numbers. Weighted-ball throwing, using balls heavier or lighter than a regulation baseball, is one of the tools most linked to this approach.

The athletes in this world are mostly older players with mature bodies, trained under staff supervision. That matters when we ask what transfers to a 10-year-old.

What the research says about weighted balls

They are common. A 2023 survey of U.S. high school pitchers found that about 63% had used a weighted-ball program (Bowman et al., 2023).

They can add velocity, with a cost in at least one study. Reinold and colleagues (2018) put 38 high school-age pitchers through either a 6-week weighted-ball program or their normal routine. The weighted-ball group gained a small but real amount of velocity. It also had more injuries: 24% of that group was hurt during the study, while the control group had none. The weighted-ball group also gained shoulder external rotation, a range-of-motion change the authors flagged as a possible injury concern.

The evidence is thin. Studies are small, the programs differ from one another, and few tracked injuries over time. We found no good studies of weighted-ball training in children under high school age.

For youth coaches: Weighted-ball and velocity programs have not been shown to be safe for young, still-growing arms. Do not run them with your team. If a family is interested, send them to a qualified professional.

What does apply to youth baseball

The useful idea for a volunteer coach is not a drill. It is the habit of measuring instead of guessing. The most important number for a youth pitcher is not velocity. It is pitch count, plus days of rest.

MLB and USA Baseball's Pitch Smart guidelines for ages 9 to 12 are the measuring stick: daily pitch limits, required rest days, at least 4 months off from throwing each year, fastballs and changeups only, and a focus on athleticism and enjoyment. Our arm care article lists the numbers.

For general strength at this age, keep it bodyweight-first: crawling, jumping, sprinting, and playing other sports. During growth spurts, cut back on jumping and bounding volume.

Who to send families to

If a player wants to throw harder, fix mechanics, or start a throwing program, the right people are a sports medicine physician, a licensed physical therapist, or a certified athletic trainer who works with throwing athletes. A good professional will look at the child's age, growth, and workload before anything else.

Try this at practice

  • Track the number that matters. Keep a pitch count and rest date for every pitcher, every game. Share it with parents.
  • Leave the radar gun at home. At rec ages, praise strikes, competing, and effort, not miles per hour.
  • Play an athletic game instead of an arm drill. Five minutes of tag, relay races, or bear-crawl races builds movement skill with no throwing load.
  • Spread the innings. Let many kids pitch a little rather than a few kids pitch a lot.
  • Have a referral ready. Know one local sports medicine physician or physical therapist who works with throwers, so you can answer “Should my kid do a velocity program?” with a name.

Guidelines & sources

  • MLB and USA Baseball (current). Pitch Smart guidelines, ages 9 to 12. Pitch limits, rest days, 4 months off from throwing, fastballs and changeups.
  • Reinold, M. M., et al. (2018). Effect of a 6-week weighted baseball throwing program on pitch velocity, pitching arm biomechanics, passive range of motion, and injury rates. Sports Health. 38 high school-age pitchers; velocity went up, and 24% of the weighted-ball group was injured versus none in controls. Small sample.
  • Bowman, E. N., et al. (2023). Most high school baseball pitchers are using weighted ball throwing programs to increase ball velocity. JSES Reviews, Reports, and Techniques. About 63% of surveyed high school pitchers had used weighted balls. Survey, one season.

Note on certainty: Research on weighted-ball and velocity training is limited to small studies of high school and older players, with mixed injury findings. There is no good evidence on these methods in children, which is exactly why we recommend against them for youth teams and point families to qualified professionals.
