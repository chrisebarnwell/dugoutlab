For Moms & Dads

The Sideline That Helps

4 min read · Written for parents, useful for coaches

The most useful thing you can say to your kid at a game costs nothing and needs no baseball knowledge. It is six words, said after the game, before anything else.

“I love to watch you play.”

That line comes from Bruce Brown and Rob Miller of Proactive Coaching. For years they asked college athletes what a parent ever said that made them feel great around a game. This was the answer that kept coming back. It was informal asking, not a controlled study. But it lines up with what researchers hear when they ask young athletes directly: kids want a parent who is supportive and glad to be there, not a second coach.

What helps from the stands

Cheer effort, not only results. “Way to battle!” after a long at-bat that ends in a groundout counts as much as “Nice hit!” Effort is the part your kid controls. In focus groups with 36 girls aged 12 to 15 in team sports, Camilla Knight and colleagues found athletes wanted parents to focus on effort over outcome.

Cheer the whole team. The same athletes asked parents to encourage everyone, not just their own kid. It takes the spotlight off your child and lets them be one of the team.

Keep a calm, warm face. Your kid will look for you in the stands. They are not checking your baseball IQ. They are checking whether you look relaxed and happy to be there.

Go easy on the car ride. Kate Tamminen and colleagues interviewed athletes and parents about the ride home. Kids were more open to talking when the parent checked how they were feeling first and when it was a two-way conversation, not a review. Many sport parenting guides suggest waiting until the next day to talk about the game. That is a sensible rule of thumb, not a research finding. If you do talk, ask instead of tell: “Anything you want to work on this week?” A shrug is a fine answer.

The whole job: during the game, cheer effort and good plays by anyone, and keep your face calm. After the game, lead with “I love to watch you play.” Save any analysis for later, and let your kid bring it up.

The one habit worth dropping

It is the one that feels most helpful: calling out instructions during play. “Get in front of it!” “Throw it to first!” “Swing!”

Two reasons to let it go.

Your kid is already busy. A nine-year-old at shortstop with a runner on first has to read the ball, get to it, remember the force is at second, and make the throw. Kids hold less in working memory than adults do, and a shout from the bleachers is one more thing to process in a second they do not have. We do not have a study that measures what a parent's shout does to a youth fielder mid-play. This is a reasonable inference, not a proven effect.

Two voices is one too many. The girls in the Knight study said it plainly: parents who coach from the stands end up contradicting what the coach told them. When the parent says “throw it home” and the coach said “get the sure out at first,” the kid has to pick a boss in the middle of the play. Let the coach be the one voice on the field during the game. You get the better job anyway.

Finding your own calm

If your chest tightens at your kid's games, you are not a bad parent. You are a caring one. It helps to name it: this is one game in a childhood full of them.

Try one slow breath out, longer than the breath in, before you react to a play. Slow breathing with a long exhale is a simple calming tool many people use, and there is some early research in adults behind it. Your job in the stands is simpler than the coach's. You do not have to fix anything. You get to enjoy watching your kid, which is what they most want from you.

Try this at the next game

  • Pick your six words ahead of time. “I love to watch you play.” Say them first after the game, win or lose, before anything else.
  • Cheer three kids who are not yours. Use their names. “Nice battle, Sam!”
  • Swap every instruction for a cheer. If “get in front of it” is on your lips, say “let's go, Tigers” instead.
  • On the ride home, ask one question and stop. “What was the most fun part today?” Then let them steer.

If you are the coach

Spend ten minutes at your first parent meeting on this. Explain the why: one voice on the field during games, and parents whose job is to cheer. Hand out the four items above. Ask parents to save technique questions for you after the game, away from the kids. Most parents want to help. Give them a clear way to do it.

Guidelines & sources

  • Brown, B., & Miller, R. (Proactive Coaching). “I love to watch you play,” the answer college athletes most often gave when asked what a parent said that made them feel great. Informal interviews over many years, not a published study.
  • Knight, C. J., Neely, K. C., & Holt, N. L. (2011). Parental behaviors in team sports: How do female athletes want parents to behave? Journal of Applied Sport Psychology. Focus groups with 36 girls aged 12 to 15; they wanted parents to encourage the whole team, focus on effort, and not give technical or tactical advice during games. Small sample, girls only, mostly non-baseball sports.
  • Omli, J., & Wiese-Bjornstal, D. M. (2011). Kids speak: Preferred parental behavior at youth sport events. Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport. Children aged 7 to 14 preferred a “supportive parent” over a “demanding coach” or “crazed fan” on the sideline.
  • Tamminen, K. A., Poucher, Z. A., & Povilaitis, V. (2017). The car ride home: An interpretive examination of parent-athlete sport conversations. Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology. Athletes were more receptive when parents read their emotional state first and kept the talk two-way. Qualitative interviews.
  • Gathercole, S. E., et al. (2004). The structure of working memory from 4 to 15 years of age. Developmental Psychology. Working memory capacity grows through childhood. Not a sport study.
  • Balban, M. Y., et al. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. Cell Reports Medicine. Daily breathing with an extended exhale improved mood in adults. Early, small-scale work.

Note on certainty: What kids say they want from parents at games is fairly consistent across interview studies, but those studies are small and ask for preferences rather than measure performance. The six-word line comes from practitioner experience, not research. The working-memory reasoning about shouted instructions is an inference we find sensible, not a tested result.
