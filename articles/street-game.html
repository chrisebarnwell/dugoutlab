Field Notes · Street-Game Baseball

Let Them Run the Game

What happens when the coaches become fence posts. Ages 9-12.

5 min read

Most of us learned baseball without a coach. A ball, a bat, some kids, and an argument about whether the tree was first base. Nobody stood in a line. Nobody waited for instructions. We just played, and we figured it out.

So one night we tried to give that back. Ninety minutes on turf. The coaches had one job: be fence posts.

The setup

  • Four trios. We snake-drafted by ability and focus so the teams were close. The top kid in each trio got a handicap card.
  • Dodgeball bracket first (12 min). Best of three. Winners pick their end of the field.
  • Race block, trio vs trio (13 min). Sack race, ball on a spoon, bucket throws from 20, 30, and 40 feet. Points to the trio.
  • 3 v 3 baseball, two fields (30 min). Half diamond at 45 feet. Soft toss. No catcher. Out is a catch, or the ball beats the runner. Ghost runners. Everyone bats every inning.
  • Full field, kids decide (20 min). Two games of six or one game of twelve. Their call. Their call is the point.
  • Finale (15 min). The winning trio picks the last game.

The coach rules

No whistle. Coaches pitch only when kids ask. Games end on score, not time. And when a game dies, we don't rescue it. We ask the trio to change one rule and restart.

That last rule is the hard one. Every instinct says step in and fix it. But a game the kids fix themselves teaches more than a game we fix for them. They have to notice what is broken, agree on a change, and live with it.

What we watched for (and didn't fix)

  • Who called fly balls without being told.
  • Who picked the 40-foot bucket over the easy one.
  • Who got loudest when his trio won.

None of that shows up in a drill line. It shows up when kids own the game.

Why it works

Play builds the base. Research on how athletes develop describes “deliberate play” in the early years: games kids organize and adapt themselves, for fun. It is linked to staying in sport longer, though most of that evidence looks back at athletes' histories (Côté).

Choice feeds motivation. Kids who feel some control over what they do tend to stay more motivated. Letting them pick the game and change the rules is a cheap way to hand them that control (Deci and Ryan, self-determination theory).

Small games mean more touches. Three on three means every kid is in every play. No right fielder picking dandelions.

Try it

  • Pick one practice this month. Make the coaches fence posts.
  • Run small games. Let the kids pick at least one of them.
  • When a game dies, ask them to change one rule. Then shut up.

Guidelines & sources this note follows

  • Côté, J., Baker, J., and Abernethy, B. (2007). Developmental Model of Sport Participation. Deliberate play in the early years. Mostly retrospective evidence.
  • Deci, E. L., and Ryan, R. M. (2000). Self-determination theory. Autonomy, competence, and relatedness support motivation.

Note on certainty: this is one practice we ran, not a study. The research supports the general ideas, play and choice, not this exact format.
