Practice Design

Let the Game Teach the Game

5 min read

Here are two ways to teach an infielder to charge a slow roller. One: explain it. “Come get it, don't wait on it.” Two: put a batter-runner at home plate who takes off when you roll the ball. If the fielder waits back, the runner is safe. Nobody has to say a word. The runner delivers the lesson.

The second version is the heart of the constraints-led approach. Change the task or the setting so the behavior you want becomes the one that works.

Where the idea comes from

The model starts with Karl Newell (1986). He described movement as shaped by three kinds of constraints acting together. Keith Davids, Chris Button, Simon Bennett, Ian Renshaw and colleagues later built that model into a coaching approach. Rob Gray, a perception and action researcher who has studied baseball hitting, has written about applying it to baseball and other sports.

  • Individual (organism) constraints. The player: size, strength, skill, confidence.
  • Environment constraints. The world around them: field, surface, light, noise.
  • Task constraints. The rules of the activity: space, number of players, ball type, how you score.

We cannot change a player's body by Saturday. We can change the task and the setting in seconds, with the gear already in the bag.

The design question: What rule, space, or scoring change would make the right play the winning play?

Four examples from a Little League practice

Hitters chasing bad pitches. Instead of saying “be selective,” play a scoring game during front toss. The coach mixes strikes and balls. Hitter earns 1 point for hard contact on a strike and 1 point for taking a ball. Swinging at a ball costs a point. The score rewards good decisions, not just contact.

Infielders waiting on slow rollers. Add the batter-runner race above. Roll or tap slow balls. Runner goes on contact. Fielder has to charge and throw to first in time. Move the runner's start closer to first to make it harder, farther back to make it easier.

Nobody calling pop-ups. New rule: a fly ball caught without a clear, loud call (“Ball, ball, ball!” or “I got it!”) does not count as an out, and the runner takes a base. Talking becomes part of making the play.

Two outs, runners frozen on contact. Set up situations with two outs and runners on base. With two outs, runners go on contact. Award the offense a point for every runner who breaks the moment the ball is hit. Take one away for a runner who hesitates.

Why it works (the theory)

The approach leans on ecological psychology, especially James Gibson's idea of “affordances”: the chances for action that a situation offers. A slow roller with a fast runner offers one action. A slow roller with nobody running offers another. The theory says players learn to see these chances and link what they see to what they do. That link is called perception-action coupling. It is built mainly by doing the skill in realistic situations, not by hearing about it.

That does not mean coaches never talk. Short cues and questions still help, especially for beginners. It means we try changing the task first, and add words second.

Vary the conditions

The approach also favors variety: different distances, speeds, spaces, and rules, so players find solutions that hold up when conditions change. Nikolai Bernstein called this “repetition without repetition.” Kids still get lots of reps. Each rep is just a little different.

What the evidence says, and what it does not

Honest version: this approach is well built on theory, but the head-to-head evidence is thin. A 2025 systematic review compared game-based (nonlinear) coaching with traditional drill-based (linear) coaching in youth team sports. It found only nine studies, most of them small and most in soccer. For technical skills like passing and ball control, about two thirds of the results showed no difference between the two. For tactical skills, meaning reading the play and choosing what to do, game-based coaching came out modestly ahead.

There are no solid studies of this in youth baseball. So we do not claim games beat drills across the board. The fair read: games look at least as good for technique, probably better for decisions, and kids usually enjoy them more. That last part matters, because kids who enjoy it come back.

Where drills still fit

Games first does not mean games only. Straight reps make sense when:

  • The skill is brand new. A kid who has never caught a fly ball needs simple, repeated tosses before a live game means anything.
  • Safety is involved. Learning to get the glove up on a ball coming at the face, or where to stand when a teammate is swinging, should be taught directly.
  • A kid is stuck and frustrated. A few easy, successful reps can rebuild confidence before going back into the game.

Then move the skill into a game as soon as it starts to hold. Drill to get it started. Play to make it work in a game.

Try this at practice

  • Pick one problem. Choose the one thing that cost you most in the last game. Write one rule or scoring change that rewards the fix.
  • Run the slow roller race. Batter-runner vs. infielder, 10 minutes. Adjust the runner's start so the fielder wins about half the time or a bit more.
  • Use the “call it or it doesn't count” rule in every fly ball activity this week.
  • Hold your first cue. Before you give an instruction, change one thing about the task and watch three reps. Then decide if you still need to say anything.
  • Change one variable per round. Distance, speed, or the scoring rule. Not all three.

Guidelines & sources

  • Newell, K. M. (1986). Constraints on the development of coordination. In Motor Development in Children (Wade & Whiting, eds.). Origin of the individual, environment, task model.
  • Davids, K., Button, C., & Bennett, S. (2008). Dynamics of Skill Acquisition: A Constraints-Led Approach. Human Kinetics. The core text for the coaching approach.
  • Renshaw, I., Davids, K., Newcombe, D., & Roberts, W. (2019). The Constraints-Led Approach: Principles for Sports Coaching and Practice Design. Routledge. Practical coaching guide.
  • Gray, R. (2021). How We Learn to Move. Accessible overview for coaches, from a researcher who has studied baseball hitting.
  • Gibson, J. J. (1979). The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception. Source of the affordance concept.
  • Bromilow, L., Milne, N., Woods, C. T., Dowsett, C. K., & Keogh, J. W. L. (2025). The effectiveness of linear and nonlinear pedagogical approaches in team-invasion ball sports: A systematic review. Sports Medicine - Open, 11, 90. Nine studies, mostly soccer, many small with some risk of bias. No clear difference for technique; modest edge for game-based coaching on tactical skill.
  • Bernstein, N. A. (1967). The Co-ordination and Regulation of Movements. Source of “repetition without repetition.”

Note on certainty: The constraints model is well established as a way to describe movement. Evidence that constraints-led coaching beats traditional drills is limited: a 2025 review found no clear difference for technique and a modest edge for decision-making, from a small set of mostly soccer studies. Nothing controlled exists yet in youth baseball. The games above are our applications of the principles, not tested protocols.
