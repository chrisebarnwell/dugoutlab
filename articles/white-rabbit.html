You Can't Outrun the White Rabbit

Teaching game tempo with a stopwatch. Ages 9-12.

5 min read

Ask a 9-year-old how long it takes a runner to get to first base and you will get a shrug. Ask them after one week of this and they will say “four and a half” before you finish the question.

That number is the whole point. Every routine ground ball is a race. The ball against the runner. We call the ball the white rabbit, and the rule is simple. You can't outrun the white rabbit. If the defense reads it, fields it clean, and throws it without a hesitation, the runner loses almost every time.

The thesis

Young players do not lose routine plays because they lack skill. Many lose them because nobody ever showed them how much time they actually have. So they rush when they have time, or they relax when they don't. The fix is not a lecture. It is a stopwatch.

So every practice at this age starts the same way. Kids running, kids fielding, and a clock that tells the truth.

How we run it (about five minutes)

  • Split the team. With 12 kids, six are runners and six spread out on defense.
  • Dead ball. We use a soft, squishy ball, not a real one. A bad throw at a runner does not hurt anybody, so kids throw with full intent.
  • Coach flips, hitter swings. A soft-toss flip. The clock starts on contact.
  • Hitter runs it out, helmet on. Two runs each, averaged. The clock stops at the bag.
  • Beat yourself. Each kid sees only his own past times and tries to beat his best. Out loud, we only say the team average. We call it The Clock.
  • Defense plays it live. Read it off the bat, field it, throw to first. Out or safe, the clock decides.
  • Flip the groups. Runners become defenders. Everybody feels both sides of the race.
  • Huddle for 20 seconds. “The Clock today: 4.47. You can't outrun the white rabbit.” Then we move on. No big talk.
The runner learns that effort to first puts pressure on the defense. The defender learns what the pace of a real game feels like. Same five minutes, two lessons, nobody standing in a line.

Why the clock works

It keeps the game's time pressure in practice. A fielder in a calm drill has all day. A fielder in a game has about four and a half. Practice that keeps the real reads and real timing of the game tends to carry over better than practice that strips them out (Pinder, Davids and colleagues, representative learning design).

It trains the read, not just the glove. The ball comes off a real bat, so the defender has to read it and decide. Training the decision, not only the mechanics, is the idea behind Vickers' decision training work.

It gives kids honest feedback without a coach talking. A time is information the player gets on their own. Feedback about results helps learning, and there is evidence that too much of it, too often, can make players lean on it (Salmoni, Schmidt and Walter). So we post the times and let the kids look.

Kids race their own number, not each other. A slow runner who drops from 5.1 to 4.7 seconds is winning. Climates where kids compare against their own improvement tend to keep effort and enjoyment up (Ames, mastery climate). That is why we track every kid over the season.

Our numbers

Twelve 9-year-olds, 60-foot bases, a hand stopwatch, three sessions. Here is The Clock, our team average home to first:

The Clock (team average)
8/26: 4.60  ·  9/2: 4.59  ·  9/23: 4.47

Our fastest kid has run a 4.03. Most of the team lives between 4.3 and 4.7. So for our team, the white rabbit gives the defense about four and a half seconds. We set 4.5 as the target line on the sheet.

Some first-run to latest-run changes (names left off):

  • 5.00 to 4.57
  • 4.84 to 4.45
  • 4.84 to 4.44
  • 4.56 to 4.34

To be honest about it, not everybody got faster. A couple of kids went up a few tenths. Kids have off days, and a hand-held stopwatch has error in it. We are not claiming the drill made them faster. Maybe effort, maybe growth, maybe noise. What we can say is that every kid knows his number now, and so does the defense.

Your team will have its own Clock. Use it. Your number is the one that matters, because it is real to your kids.

What the defense learns

Four and a half seconds is not much. Read it off the bat, field it clean, throw to first. A bobble costs half a second. A hesitation costs another. On a routine play, that is usually the whole margin. Once kids feel it, you do not need to yell “hurry.” They already know.

And they also learn the flip side. Four and a half seconds is enough. A clean, routine play does not need panic. Rushing is how the ball ends up in the dugout.

Try it this week

  • One stopwatch, one soft ball, one base.
  • Five minutes at the start of practice.
  • Write the times down. Show them next week.
  • Say it once: you can't outrun the white rabbit.

Guidelines & sources this note follows

  • Pinder, R., Davids, K., Renshaw, I., and Araújo, D. (2011). Representative learning design. Practice that keeps the reads and timing of the real game transfers better.
  • Vickers, J. N. (2007). Perception, Cognition, and Decision Training. Training decisions under game-like conditions.
  • Salmoni, A. W., Schmidt, R. A., and Walter, C. B. (1984). Knowledge of results and motor learning. Results feedback helps, but constant feedback can create dependence.
  • Ames, C. (1992). Achievement goals and motivational climate. Self-referenced, mastery-focused climates support effort and enjoyment.
  • Our own times. Beach Park Ballers 9U, three sessions, 60-foot bases. Hand-timed, so expect a tenth or two of error. Not a published youth norm.

Note on certainty: none of the research above tested this exact game. We are applying general findings on practice design, feedback, and motivation to a five-minute warm-up. The timing numbers are ours. Treat this as an idea to try, not a proven method.
