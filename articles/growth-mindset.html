Growth Mindset on the Baseball Field

4 min read

Two kids boot a ground ball in the same inning. One hangs his head and stays down on himself the rest of the inning. The other shakes it off and gets back in his ready position. Same error. Very different story about what it meant.

Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck spent decades studying those stories. She called them mindsets. The idea is useful for coaches. It is also more modest than the posters make it sound, so here is what holds up and what does not.

The two mindsets, simply

A fixed mindset is the belief that ability is something you either have or you don't. An error becomes a verdict: “I'm not a good fielder.” Needing effort feels like proof you lack talent.

A growth mindset is the belief that ability is built through practice and learning. An error becomes information: “I came up too early on that one.” Effort is how you get better, not a sign that you are bad.

What the research actually shows

The best-known study is Mueller & Dweck (1998). Fifth graders who were praised for being smart chose easier tasks and persisted less after a hard set of problems than kids praised for their effort. That finding shaped a lot of classroom practice.

Later work cooled things down:

  • Two large meta-analyses (Sisk et al., 2018) found the link between mindset and school achievement was weak overall. Mindset programs helped most for students who were struggling or from low-income families.
  • A national study of over 12,000 ninth graders (Yeager et al., 2019) found a short online mindset program raised grades for lower-achieving students by about a tenth of a grade point. Real, but small, and it worked best where the school culture backed it up.
  • A 2019 attempt to replicate the praise study in China (Li & Bates) did not find the effect. Other researchers re-analyzed the same data and argued it did support the original finding. Call it unsettled.

Almost all of this is school research, not sport. So we are not promising that mindset talk will change your team's batting average. We use it for a simpler reason: talking about what a player did, instead of what a player is, is clear feedback. It gives the kid something to do on the next pitch.

The honest takeaway: Praise the action, not the talent. It is low cost, it is clear, and it points the player at something they control. Just don't expect a one-line pep talk to transform a kid by itself.

Talent language vs. action language

Talent labels slip into everyday dugout talk. None of these are said with bad intent:

  • “He's just a natural.”
  • “You're not an infielder. You're a hitter.”
  • “Some kids are born to hit.”

Each one describes who the kid is. None tells them what to do next. Action language does:

After an error: “You came up before the ball got there. Next one, watch it all the way into your glove.” Short, specific, then move on.

After a strikeout: “Good swing on the first pitch. With two strikes, what were you looking for?” A question gets the kid thinking about the at-bat instead of the result.

After a good play: “You charged that slow roller instead of waiting on it. That's why you got the out.” Credit goes to the behavior, so the kid knows what to repeat.

A team mistake routine

Many teams use a short routine after any error. The one we suggest: the player does a quick reset (a slap of the glove, a deep breath), teammates call “next play,” and everyone gets back in ready position. The coach moves on too.

We are not aware of a study that proves a routine like this improves performance. We use it because it gives the kid something to do with the bad feeling, and it shows the whole team that errors are normal. Treat it as a good habit, not a proven fix.

The scoreboard still counts

Action language does not mean pretending results don't matter. Kids know the score. It means talking about the parts they control. “They hit the ball hard today. We made the routine plays, and we can work on getting to first base faster.” That beats any explanation built on talent, theirs or ours.

Try this at practice

  1. One action per comment. Every time you give feedback, name one thing the player did. “You got your glove down early.” Not “You're a great fielder.”
  2. Teach the reset in five minutes. Hit ground balls in a round. On any bobble, the fielder does the reset and teammates call “next play.” Practice it when stakes are zero so it shows up in games.
  3. Ask one question after an at-bat. “What pitch were you looking for?” Listen to the answer. Don't grade it.
  4. Swap one talent line. Pick a phrase you use (“natural,” “born to hit”) and replace it with the specific thing the kid did.
  5. End-of-practice check. Ask yourself: did most of my comments name an action, or just a result?

Guidelines & sources

  • Mueller, C. M., & Dweck, C. S. (1998). Praise for intelligence can undermine children's motivation and performance. Lab tasks with fifth graders, not sport.
  • Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). Two meta-analyses on growth mindset and academic achievement. Overall effects weak; some benefit for struggling and low-income students.
  • Yeager, D. S., et al. (2019). National Study of Learning Mindsets, Nature. Small grade gains for lower-achieving ninth graders, strongest in supportive school cultures.
  • Li, Y., & Bates, T. C. (2019). Did not replicate the praise-for-intelligence effect with Chinese schoolchildren. A later re-analysis of the same data disputed that conclusion.

Note on certainty: Mindset research is mostly from classrooms, and the effects are small and depend on context. There is little direct evidence from youth baseball. The mistake routine and feedback scripts here are practical coaching habits, not proven interventions.
