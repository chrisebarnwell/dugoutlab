Stories From the Dugout

The Week Before

The nerves are ours, not theirs.

5 min read

A group of kids who met a month ago, a team we barely had enough players to field, kept winning. Now every game is the biggest of their lives, and the same thought shows up in every coach and parent at once. We better take this seriously now.

That is the voice to watch. It shows up the moment things get big and says it is time to lock in. We all have it. It means well. At this age, it usually steers us wrong. So before we worry about the other team, we check our own heads first.

We have already seen it work

We were behind in the championship, in the biggest spot these girls had ever stood in. They did not tighten up. They stayed loose, kept playing, and won it.

One game does not prove anything. But it does tell us the let-them-play approach did not fall apart when the game got heavy. We watched it hold.

A confession

I will go first, because I am as guilty as anyone. This week I caught myself firing off a message to the other coaches. No chance the softball a couple districts over is any softer than what we just came through, so they had better show up ready to play the level we just beat.

Read that back. It is not a scouting report. It is a pump-up speech I was about to load onto kids who never asked for it. That was my own childhood training talking. The grind. The chip. The prove-it. Most of us were raised on that script, so the instinct is not a flaw. It is just how we learned the game. I have never once seen it help an eleven-year-old hit a softball.

And notice who the speech was for. Not the girls. They are loose. They are laughing. They came from behind and nobody had to talk them into it. The nerves the rest of us carry are tangled up with our own stuff. The kids did not bring that. We did.

The crisp practice trap

Getting serious at this age is tempting because it looks so good. Lines that move. Everybody catching. A parent watches and thinks, now there is a coach who has it together.

Here is the problem. In a drill that tidy, the kids know exactly what is coming. Stand here. Ball comes there. They never have to read anything or decide anything. Games are the opposite. A grounder does not announce itself. Every play is a read made in real time.

The messy stuff we run with Dueling Banjos blaring, where it looks like chaos, is where much of the real training happens, because that is where they have to read and choose on the fly. Practice that looks like the game tends to carry over to the game better than practice that does not (Pinder, Davids and colleagues, representative learning design; Bjork, desirable difficulties).

Same with getting doubled off on a line drive. We can explain “freeze on a liner” at a whiteboard all week. Get doubled off once in a real game and she will probably never forget it. Emotionally charged moments tend to stick in memory better than calm ones (McGaugh).

So why do we keep reaching for the tidy drills? Partly because they make us look organized. Crisp lines are often more for the coach than the kid. Ego check.

We just take shifts

There is one piece of this we cannot hold alone. We get these kids two or three hours a week. Families have them the rest. Whatever we protect at practice lives or dies in those other hours. We are all coaching the same kid. We just take shifts. So the ask, to ourselves and to every parent in the bleachers, is the same. Keep it loose. The pull to fix a swing on the drive over, to remind her how big this is, is the same one we are fighting in ourselves. It is still the wrong one.

Loose does not mean lazy

Loose is not doing nothing. We are still getting better, just in the way that works at this age.

One cue, not a lecture. Point it at the ball or the target, not her body. Research on attention consistently finds that cues aimed outside the body lead to better performance and learning than cues about body parts (Wulf).

Internal cue (less effective)
“Rotate your hips.”
External cue (more effective)
“Drive it up the middle.”

No “this is the big one” speeches. Research on choking suggests pressure can pull a player's attention onto the step-by-step mechanics of a skill she already knows, and that is when good players tighten up (Beilock and Carr). Treat it like all the others.

An afternoon that does not look like practice

This week my own kid and I are away on vacation, so I am going to miss every one of our practices. We do not have much with us. A little pink glove and one softball, the same one she has had since she started. We will still get plenty done.

Wiffle ball, where the ball moves and she has to read it every pitch. Rocks at a tree, where the target tells her how every throw went. A football, a volleyball, back and forth until we lose count. None of it is softball. Does doing it this way guarantee anything? No. Nothing does.

So what does it give her? An afternoon solving movement problems with her own body across a dozen kinds of ball and throw. Research on early sampling links playing lots of sports and games to staying in sport longer and developing more broadly, though most of that evidence looks back at athletes' histories rather than testing it directly (Côté). And she comes home still loving this. Still wanting to play tomorrow. That want is the engine, and we will have spent the afternoon feeding it instead of grinding it down.

What we are really asking

The bracket is going to get louder. The job, for all of us, is to stay exactly the same. Same energy. Same car rides. Same fun. One cue when a cue is needed. No fixing, no drilling for show, no big-moment speeches.

We are not guessing. We already watched them come from behind and win doing it exactly this way. We do not need a new plan for a bigger stage. We need the nerve to run the same one.

And if anything travels from the bleachers to the box this week, let it be six words. When college athletes were asked what a parent said that made them feel best, this is the answer that kept coming up. Not a tip. Not a fix. I love to watch you play (Brown and Miller). Watch them. Say it. Mean it. That is the whole assignment.

Guidelines & sources this reflection follows

  • Pinder, R., Davids, K., Renshaw, I., and Araújo, D. (2011). Representative learning design. Practice that keeps the reads and decisions of the real game transfers better. Popularized for coaches by Rob Gray.
  • Bjork, R. (1994). Desirable difficulties. Harder, messier practice often looks worse in the moment but builds more durable skill.
  • McGaugh, J. L. (2004). Emotional arousal and memory. Emotionally charged moments are remembered better than neutral ones.
  • Wulf, G. (2013). Attentional focus and motor learning, a review. Cue toward the ball or target, not the body.
  • Beilock, S. L., and Carr, T. H. (2001). Choking under pressure. Lab studies with adults; applied here to kids by extension.
  • Côté, J. Developmental Model of Sport Participation. Early sampling tied to longer participation. Mostly retrospective evidence.
  • Brown, B., and Miller, R. Proactive Coaching. “I love to watch you play” from informal interviews with college athletes. Not a controlled study.

Note on certainty: external focus has the strongest support of the claims here. The choking and memory research was done mostly with adults. The sampling research is mostly backward-looking. Brown and Miller is an informal survey. We hold all of it as guidance, not gospel.
