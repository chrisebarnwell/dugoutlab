Four Stations, Nobody Sits

A real four-station rotation with the game built in. Ages 9-12.

5 min read

Twelve kids, four stations, three kids in each. Five or six minutes per station, then rotate. The rule behind all of it: nobody sits around.

This is a real rotation we ran. One station is plain traditional work. The other three are built so the game's timing and reads are already in them.

Station 1: Outfield (traditional)

One coach, three kids. Drop steps and getting behind the ball. Nothing fancy. Some fundamentals are worth a few clean reps before they go back into a game, and that is fine.

Station 2: Infield, the four-second count

  • One coach flips, another coach hits a ground ball to the kid at shortstop.
  • The kid waiting in line counts out loud: “One Mississippi, two Mississippi, three Mississippi…”
  • The third kid plays first base. After a few reps, they rotate.

The count is the point. Our runners get to first in about four and a half seconds, so the fielder hears the clock running the whole play. (More on that in You Can't Outrun the White Rabbit.)

We always put a kid at first, not a coach or a net. A real target the size of a player is what they will throw to in a game, so that is what they practice throwing to.

Stations 3 and 4: Ball in the dirt (run together)

These two stations share the same pitches.

Station 3, runners at third. A third base coach reminds them: be aggressive on a ball in the dirt. Watch the ball in flight and look for the angle that says it is going to bounce. We want kids to start seeing the flight of the pitch at a young age, so they get an early jump.

Station 4, the battery. One kid in a catcher's mask and chest protector, receiving and blocking. His only job: don't let it get past you. One kid hits. One kid umpires and calls strikes. Every minute and a half or so, they rotate catcher, hitter, umpire.

So every ball in the dirt is a live race. The runner is reading the angle and going. The catcher is trying to keep it in front of him. Both sides get real feedback on the same pitch.

We use soft “dead” balls here. The catcher doesn't get beat up, the hitter doesn't get hurt, and neither does the kid playing umpire.

Why we build it this way

Small groups mean more reps. Three kids per station means nobody waits long. Lines of nine kids waiting for one ground ball teach mostly waiting.

Keep the game's timing and targets in the drill. Counting out loud and throwing to a real player keep the timing and the target of the game in the rep. Practice that keeps those pieces tends to carry over better (Pinder, Davids and colleagues, representative learning design).

Practice the read, not just the run. Reading a pitch's angle and deciding to go is a decision. Decisions get better with practice in game-like conditions (Vickers, decision training).

Pair skills that meet in a game. The runner and the catcher are on opposite sides of the same play. Running them together means each one practices against the real thing, not against a coach pretending.

Try it

  • Groups of three. Five to six minutes per station.
  • Put a kid at first, not a coach or a net.
  • Have the kid in line count out loud on every ground ball.
  • Pair your baserunners with your catchers so every ball in the dirt is live.

Guidelines & sources this note follows

  • Pinder, R., Davids, K., Renshaw, I., and Araújo, D. (2011). Representative learning design.
  • Vickers, J. N. (2007). Perception, Cognition, and Decision Training.
  • Equipment. Catchers wear a mask and chest protector. Soft balls in the battery station.

Note on certainty: this is one rotation from one 9U team. The station design is ours. The research supports keeping game timing and decisions in practice, not this exact layout.
