Field Notes · Aggressive Baserunning

Send ’Em

Find the far edge of aggressive, then learn from it. Ages 9-12.

5 min read

One night our message to every coach was simple. If you are coaching first or third: send ’em.

Steal. Go on any ball in the dirt. Take the extra base every time. Let the boys find the far edge of aggressive and feel what it does to the other team.

Why on purpose

Baserunning effort is one of the few things a team controls completely. You can't control the pitch or the hop. You can control how hard you go and whether you go.

Most young players are too cautious, not too bold. They freeze on a ball in the dirt because nobody ever let them find out what happens if they run. Practice is the place to find out, because getting thrown out costs nothing there.

What we saw

Kids got thrown out. Some of them a lot. That was fine. Every out at second taught them a little more about where the line is: how far a ball has to get away, how strong the catcher's arm is, when to go and when to hold.

And the defense learned too. When the other team runs on everything, you have to make the play, every time. Pressure on offense is also a gift to the defense.

Being too aggressive in practice is how you find out where aggressive ends. You can't learn the edge from the safe side of it.

The research angle

Mistakes can help learning. Errors followed by feedback can improve learning, especially when the learner was trying something with intent (Metcalfe, learning from errors). A kid thrown out going hard gets instant, honest feedback from the game itself.

Decisions need reps. Reading when to go is a decision. Decisions get better when they are practiced in game-like conditions, not explained at a whiteboard (Vickers, decision training).

Try it

  • Tell your base coaches: send ’em, all practice.
  • Do not punish outs. Ask one question after: “What did you see?”
  • In games, dial it back to smart. They will now know where smart is.

Guidelines & sources this note follows

  • Metcalfe, J. (2017). Learning from errors. Annual Review of Psychology. Errors with corrective feedback can enhance learning. Mostly classroom research.
  • Vickers, J. N. (2007). Perception, Cognition, and Decision Training.

Note on certainty: the error research is mostly from classrooms, not ball fields. We are applying it by extension. This is a practice idea, not a proven method.
