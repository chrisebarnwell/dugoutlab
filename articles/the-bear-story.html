A Confession

The Bear Story

6 min read · How I was built for war — and why it capped my career.

When I was nine years old, my little league coach told us a story about wrestling a bear.

His name was Jim Ray. He told us he had no money for books, so he went to a fair and got in the ring with a bear, and the bear threw him out, and he climbed back in. That was the whole story. Not about strategy. Not about the bear. About getting thrown out and climbing back in. About refusing to quit even when something three times your size is telling you to.

We were nine. We sat in a circle and we lit up. I lit up. Whatever pilot light a coach can light in a kid, Jim Ray lit it in me that day.

I loved Jim. I still do. He was wrong about baseball, and I didn't know it for twenty years.

I was small. Undersized. Played at a NAIA college nobody had heard of. By every scouting metric I had no business in pro ball. But I had the pilot light. I played angry. I played hard. I played like the bear was always in the ring with me. And it worked just enough. The Brewers drafted me.

My first year of pro ball, 2001, fate did a thing. Jim Ray, the man who lit the pilot light when I was nine, had just relocated to Ogden, Utah. That's where I was assigned. I moved in with him. The man who built me for war was now the man making me dinner at the start of my professional career.

I struggled out of the gate. Small kid from a small school, suddenly in pro ball, trying to live up to something. My swing left me. My confidence left me. Jim was patient. He watched me press and flail for weeks.

Until he wasn't patient anymore.

One night after another bad game, Jim walked over to the dugout and told me to get my ass to the car. He was sick of watching it. On the drive home he laid into me about pissing away an opportunity. He was right. He was also, without knowing it, talking to the nine-year-old kid in the circle who first heard the bear story. He relit the pilot light. He told the nine-year-old in me to climb back in the ring.

A few nights later, the explosion came.

We were playing the Casper Rockies. Tied for first. August 2001. Down 2-1 in the fifth. Bases loaded. Carlos Soriano lined a single to right. The runner from third tagged. I was on second. I came around third and I saw the catcher, James Sweeney, set up at the plate with the ball coming in.

I ran him over.

He went down. The ball came loose. I was safe with the tying run and a broken nose. I finished the game. After the game they took me to the hospital.

The next morning my name was in the paper. The manager called it a Rocky movie. I finished that year hitting .306. I won the coaches award. The Brewers named me their most competitive player in the organization.

The pilot light worked. The bear-wrestling story worked. Jim Ray was right.

That's what I believed for a long time.

Here is what took me twenty years to understand.

I ran a man over. He was a 22-year-old kid named James Sweeney, who was trying to do his job, and I ran him over because Jim Ray told me a bear story when I was nine and because Jim Ray told me to get to the car a few nights earlier and because the only way I knew how to play the game was on fire.

And the world rewarded me for it. Trophies. Headlines. A nickname. Awards. Every reward I ever got in baseball, I got for feeding the beast. So I kept feeding it.

The beast got me to pro ball. The beast did not get me where I wanted to go after that. Because at every level above that, the players were just as talented and a lot of them were calm. The calm ones won. The angry one ran catchers over and made the paper and didn't move up.

Baseball isn't war. Jim Ray loved me, and he was wrong. The calm, confident, poised player wins. The angry one breaks his nose and calls it courage and gets a clipping for the scrapbook and wonders later why he couldn't get past where he got.

I coach 7, 8, and 9-year-olds now. I coached my daughter for years. She's 12. She had the same nerves I had. She stuffed them down the way I stuffed them down. I didn't see it for a long time because I was still stuffing mine.

The kid who heard the bear story is still in my dugout.
