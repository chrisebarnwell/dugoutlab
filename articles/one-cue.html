Coaching Cues

The One Cue Rule: Say Less, Say It Clearly

4 min read

TL;DR The short version

  • The idea: A swing is over in a fraction of a second, and young kids hold less in working memory. Give one cue per rep; a new cue replaces the old one. No study tests this in youth baseball; it is a principle, not a proven law.
  • What the research shows: Target cues (“hit it up the middle”) often beat body-part cues in studies, but a 2024 reanalysis found the benefit may be small or near zero. They are short and low-risk, so use them by default.
  • Try this: Write each station's one cue on wrist tape, five words or fewer, and have the player say it back.

Every rec coach has done it. A kid steps in, and we fire off four things before the pitch: “Bend your knees, eye on the ball, don't drop your elbow, follow through.” The kid swings and misses. Did any of it land?

Probably not much of it. A swing takes a fraction of a second. There is no time to run a checklist.

Why one cue

Working memory, the mental scratchpad we hold instructions in, is small. It is smaller still in young children and grows through childhood into the teen years. Ask a 9-year-old to hold four instructions while reacting to a moving ball and most of them will drop.

We could not find a study that directly tests one cue against three in youth baseball. The one-cue rule is a coaching principle built on what we know about attention and memory, plus a lot of practical experience. We think it is sound. It is not a proven law.

In plain English: One clear cue has a better chance of landing than three. Our job is to pick the one that matters most right now.

Where the cue points

The second choice is where the cue points attention.

Internal cues point at a body part: “Keep your elbow up,” “Bend your knees,” “Turn your hips.”

External cues point at the ball, a target, or the result: “Hit it back up the middle,” “Throw it to his chest,” “Stay low like you're sliding under a table.”

Internal cue
“Keep your elbow up.”
External cue
“Hit a line drive back at the pitcher's screen.”

Gabriele Wulf at UNLV has led decades of research on this. Many studies, and a large 2021 review she co-authored, found external cues tend to produce better performance and learning. A 2024 reanalysis by Brad McKay and colleagues pushed back hard. After correcting for publication bias, they found the average benefit may be small or close to zero.

Our take: external cues are unlikely to hurt, they are shorter, and they give kids something concrete to aim at. Use them as the default. Do not expect magic.

How to use it at practice

Before a drill starts, pick one cue. When kids run the drill, that is the only thing you say. If a different problem shows up, save it for a later rep or a later drill. Do not stack it on top.

  • Fielding: “Beat the ball to the spot.” Not “Get in front, glove down, watch it in.”
  • Playing catch: “Hit your partner in the chest.” Not a list of body positions. (We do not coach throwing or pitching mechanics on this site. For those, work with a qualified instructor.)
  • Hitting: “Hit it up the middle.” Not “See the ball, hands back, rotate your hips.”

The cue you cut will feel urgent. That is fine. Picking one forces us to say the thing that matters most right now.

When something is really wrong

Stop the rep. Give one correction. Start a new rep. If the new problem matters more than the old one, the new cue replaces the old one. It does not get added to it.

The rule: One cue per rep. A new cue replaces the old one. Never stack.

For T-ball coaches

With 4 to 6 year olds, keep it even simpler. Pick one cue for the whole practice, not one per drill. “Swing hard with two hands” works. “Hit the ball off the tee to the grass” works. Say it before every swing and let everything else go.

Try this at practice

  • Write it on tape. Before each station, write the one cue on athletic tape on your wrist. That is all you say at that station.
  • Swap body cues for targets. Take one body-part cue you use a lot and rewrite it as a target or result. Try it for a week.
  • Ask them to repeat it. After you give the cue, ask the player to say it back. If they can't, it was too long.
  • Count your words. Aim for cues of five words or fewer.
  • Hold the rest. Jot other fixes on a notecard for next practice instead of saying them now.
Try this with your kid

Next time you play catch, pick one target cue, like “hit me in the chest,” and make it the only thing you say. Ask your kid to say it back. Save any other fixes for another day.

Guidelines & sources

  • Gathercole, S. E., Pickering, S. J., Ambridge, B., & Wearing, H. (2004). The structure of working memory from 4 to 15 years of age. Developmental Psychology. Working memory grows steadily through childhood. Not a sport study.
  • Wulf, G. (2013). Attentional focus and motor learning: a review of 15 years. International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology. Summary of external-focus research.
  • Chua, L.-K., Jimenez-Diaz, J., Lewthwaite, R., Kim, T., & Wulf, G. (2021). Superiority of external attentional focus for motor performance and learning. Psychological Bulletin. Meta-analyses favoring external focus.
  • McKay, B., Corson, A. E., et al. (2024). Reporting bias, not external focus. Psychological Bulletin. After bias correction, average effects were small to nil.

Note on certainty: The one-cue rule is a practical principle, not a tested finding; no study we found compares cue counts in youth baseball. The external-focus advantage is debated, and recent reanalysis suggests it is smaller than once claimed. Both ideas are low-risk to try.
