The Philosophy Behind DugoutLab

Your One Job as a Youth Coach

6 min read · Featured

TL;DR The short version

  • The idea: The coach's job is to make kids want to come back tomorrow. Skill takes years, and none of it happens if a kid quits.
  • Why it matters: In one small Little League study, players of coaches trained in supportive coaching dropped out at 5% the next year, versus 26%. In a 2019 parent survey, the top reason kids quit was that it stopped being fun. This is the site's reading of several studies, not one proof.
  • Try this: Before practice ends, ask: Did everyone move? How did it end? Did every kid hear one specific thing? Did anyone fail most of the time? Act on it next time.

This site has drills, articles, a practice framework, and a season plan. They draw on researchers who study how kids learn, how they stay motivated, and how they stay healthy.

All of it points to the same place.

The DugoutLab Philosophy
“Your job is to make them want to come back tomorrow.”

Not win the championship. Not build the next pro. Not cram the most skill into one practice. Make them want to come back tomorrow.

Skill takes years. It builds season after season. None of that happens if the player stops showing up. You can't develop a kid who isn't there.

What the research points to

The researchers behind this site came at youth sport from different angles: motor learning, psychology, injury prevention. None of them set out to answer “will this kid come back?” But their findings fit together.

Gabriele Wulf
Cues that point attention outside the body (the ball, the target) tend to help people learn and perform better than cues about body parts. Most of this work is with adults, and a 2024 reanalysis found the average effect may be small. It is cheap to try, so we use it.
Guadagnoli & Lee
Learning is best when practice difficulty fits the player. Too easy and they coast. Too hard and they fail over and over. The “about 7 in 10” target coaches use is a rule of thumb built on this idea.
Keith Davids
Shape the practice (space, rules, equipment) and players find solutions themselves instead of being told every step. This is the constraints-led approach. We use it as a lens, not a proven winner: studies comparing games and drills show a modest edge for games on decision skill and mostly no difference for technique. So we go games first, with drills where they help.
Carol Dweck
How adults talk about effort and mistakes can shape how kids see their own ability. Later studies found these effects are real but small, and mostly measured in classrooms.
Joan Vickers
Decision-making can be trained. Practice that asks players to read the situation and choose builds that skill, not just repetition of a set drill.
Smoll & Smith
In a Little League study, players of coaches trained in supportive, encouraging coaching dropped out at 5% the next year. Players of untrained coaches dropped out at 26%. Win-loss records did not explain it.
Ryan & Deci
Self-determination theory: people stick with activities where they feel competent, connected to others, and have some choice. Practice can build all three.
Fleisig & Andrews
In a 10-year study of youth pitchers, those who threw more than 100 innings in a year were about 3.5 times more likely to be seriously hurt. Pitch counts and rest keep kids on the field.
AAP (Brenner)
Specializing in one sport early is linked to more overuse injuries and burnout, with no clear evidence it makes better athletes. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests delaying specialization until about age 15 or 16.

Put those together and one thing stands out. Better cues, better practice design, and better decisions all matter. Every one of them depends on the player still being there next season.

What getting it wrong looks like

A 9U season with a great record and three kids who never sign up again is not a win. Those kids' baseball stops at 9. Whatever they might have become, we won't find out.

It happens a lot. A 2019 Aspen Institute and Utah State survey found the average child plays a sport for under three years and quits around age 11. The top reason parents gave: it wasn't fun anymore.

When researchers asked youth players what makes sport fun, the top answers were being a good sport, trying hard, and a positive coach. Winning landed 48th of 81, and cool uniforms and gear came last.

That comes from Amanda Visek's FUN MAPS study (2015). Adults tend to focus on the scoreboard. Kids are telling us they care more about how the team plays together and how the coach treats them.

What getting it right looks like

It looks like ending practice with something that makes them laugh. A coach who kneels to eye level when a kid is crying instead of standing over them. A “next play” routine after errors that the team runs without thinking.

It looks like asking “Where's the play?” before pitches, as an invitation to think, not a quiz. Small-sided games where every kid touches the ball instead of standing in one line. A parent-coach who says “I love to watch you play” on the ride home and leaves it there.

It looks like a pitcher who ends the season with a healthy arm because the coach followed pitch count and rest rules, even when the playoff game was close and the kid wanted to stay in.

It looks like a T-ball player who crosses home on the last play of practice and turns around grinning. That kid comes back. And comes back again. At 15, bigger and stronger, they are still around to keep learning.

Showing up adds up

A player who starts at 6 and plays until 16 gets ten seasons of practices, games, and backyard catch. A player who quits at 11 gets five. Whatever the gap looked like at 9, the kid who stays has five more years to get better. The kid who left has none.

That is the job. Drills, practice plans, and cues are tools. The job is what the tools are for: making them want to come back tomorrow.

Try this at practice

Ask yourself four questions before practice ends. Then act on them next time.

  1. Did everyone move? Or did some kids stand in a line for 40 minutes? If so, split into three groups next time (hitting, fielding, a game) and rotate every 8 to 10 minutes.
  2. How did it end? Kids carry the end of practice to the car. If it ended with sprints or a lecture, change it. Finish with a game: pickle, a wiffle ball scrimmage, or a baserunning relay.
  3. Did every kid hear one specific thing? Not “good job.” Something you saw: “You charged that slow roller instead of waiting on it. That's how you make that play.” Thirty seconds each. Kids who know the coach watched them feel like they matter.
  4. Did anyone fail most of the time? If a drill was too hard for a kid, make it easier next time: closer, slower, bigger target.
The only metric that matters
Did they leave wanting to come back tomorrow?

If yes, you did your job. The skills, the wins, and the long-term development need time. That time only exists if they come back.

That is the whole philosophy. It is why this site exists. Not to make you a better drill designer, but to help you build a practice where a kid leaves on Saturday morning thinking this is the best part of the week.

Go do that.

Guidelines & sources

  • Wulf, G. (2013). Attentional focus and motor learning: a review of 15 years. Review favoring external focus; most studies use adults.
  • McKay, B., Corson, A. E., et al. (2024). Reanalysis in Psychological Bulletin. After correcting for publication bias, the average external focus effect was small to nil.
  • Guadagnoli, M. A., & Lee, T. D. (2004). Challenge point framework. Difficulty should fit the learner. Does not specify a 70% target.
  • Renshaw, I., Davids, K., Newcombe, D., & Roberts, W. (2019). The Constraints-Led Approach. Practical book on designing practice through task and environment.
  • Bromilow, L., Milne, N., Woods, C. T., Dowsett, C. K., & Keogh, J. W. L. (2025). Systematic review, Sports Medicine - Open, 11, 90. Nine studies, mostly soccer. No clear difference for technique; modest edge for game-based coaching on tactical skill. No youth baseball studies.
  • Mueller, C. M., & Dweck, C. S. (1998); Sisk, V. F., et al. (2018); Yeager, D. S., et al. (2019). Praise and mindset research. Later large studies found small, context-dependent effects, mostly in school settings.
  • Vickers, J. N. (2007). Perception, Cognition, and Decision Training. Decision training in sport.
  • Barnett, N. P., Smoll, F. L., & Smith, R. E. (1992). Coach Effectiveness Training and Little League attrition: 5% vs. 26% dropout. Small sample (18 coaches).
  • Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory: competence, relatedness, and autonomy support motivation.
  • Fleisig, G. S., Andrews, J. R., et al. (2011). 10-year prospective study of youth pitchers; over 100 innings a year linked to about 3.5 times higher serious injury risk. Follow MLB/USA Baseball Pitch Smart and your league's pitch count rules.
  • Brenner, J. S., & AAP Council on Sports Medicine and Fitness (2016). Sports specialization and intensive training in young athletes. Recommends delaying specialization until about 15 or 16.
  • Aspen Institute Project Play & Utah State University (2019). Parent survey: average child quits a sport around age 11; “not fun anymore” was the top reason.
  • Visek, A. J., et al. (2015). The Fun Integration Theory (FUN MAPS). Youth soccer sample in one region; winning ranked 48th of 81 fun determinants.

Note on certainty: No single study proves that “come back tomorrow” is the best way to coach. It is our reading of several lines of work: dropout research, coach-behavior studies, motivation theory, and injury data. The pitch count and specialization findings are the strongest. Mindset and focus-of-attention effects are real but smaller, and mostly from settings other than youth baseball.
