Why Kids Quit Baseball, and What You Can Control

5 min read

TL;DR The short version

  • The idea: In a survey of sports parents, the top reason kids quit was that it wasn't fun anymore. Kids, parents, and coaches tied fun to trying hard, being a good sport, and positive coaching. Winning ranked 48th of 81.
  • Why it matters: You can't control your record, but you can control practice. In one small Little League study, kids of coaches trained in supportive coaching dropped out at 5%, versus 26%.
  • Try this: Run three stations instead of one line, end with a game, and tell each kid one specific thing they did well before they leave.

Every spring, a new group of kids signs up. Every fall, some of them don't come back. You may have heard that 70% of kids quit organized sports by age 13. That number is everywhere, but we could not find the study behind it. It traces back to a figure quoted in a 2016 Washington Post article with no published data attached. So we don't use it.

Better numbers exist. A 2019 Aspen Institute and Utah State University survey of about 1,000 sports parents found the average child played a sport for under three years and quit around age 11. The most common reason parents gave: it wasn't fun anymore.

That matters for a volunteer coach. You have little control over your win-loss record. You have a lot of control over whether practice is fun.

What “fun” means to kids

Amanda Visek and colleagues (2015) asked youth soccer players, their parents, and coaches what makes sport fun. They mapped 81 things into 11 groups, called the FUN MAPS. The three groups rated most important:

  • Being a good sport
  • Trying hard
  • Positive coaching (encouragement, clear instruction, being treated with respect)

Learning and improving, games, and team friendships were on the map too. Winning was there, but only 48th of 81, well behind effort and good coaching. Cool uniforms and gear ranked last. This was one soccer sample from the Washington, D.C. area, so treat it as a strong hint, not a law. Still, it lines up with the broader research. A review of 43 dropout studies (Crane & Temple, 2015) found the reasons kids leave cluster around lack of enjoyment, feeling they aren't good enough, social pressure, and other things competing for their time.

The coaching finding: In a Little League study, players whose coaches had a short training in encouraging, supportive coaching dropped out at 5% the next season. Players with untrained coaches dropped out at 26%. Team win-loss records did not explain the difference (Barnett, Smoll & Smith, 1992). It was a small study, but it is baseball, and it points at the coach.

The standing-around problem

Picture one batting practice line for an hour with 12 kids. Each kid gets about 5 minutes at the plate and spends 55 minutes waiting or shagging balls. The kids know it. Bored kids start goofing off, and some decide baseball is boring.

The fix is more groups, not more time. Split into three groups of four. One group hits off tees or soft toss into a fence or net. One group fields live balls off the coach's fungo. One group plays a quick game like pickle (a rundown between two bases). Rotate every 8 to 10 minutes. Every kid is doing something most of the hour.

How you correct matters

You still have to correct mistakes. How you do it is the part kids remember. Being called out across the field in front of teammates is embarrassing for most 9-year-olds. A quiet word up close usually lands better.

The simple rule many coach-education programs teach: praise in public, correct in private. Walk over. Give one thing to fix. Walk away.

One thing to stop doing today: Shouting corrections from across the field. Walk up to the player instead. The fix will land better and the kid won't dread your attention.

Feeling competent

Kids who feel they are bad at something tend to drift away from it. That has a practice-design lesson: set drills so kids succeed often enough to feel progress, but not so often that it is boring.

A common rule of thumb is about 7 successes in 10 tries. That number is a coaching guideline, not a precise research finding. It comes from the broader idea that learning is best when the challenge fits the learner (Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004). If a drill fails 8 times out of 10, make it easier. If nobody ever misses, make it harder.

So if your 7-year-olds can't reach a partner from 50 feet, move them to 25. If nobody can hit live pitching, use coach pitch from closer or soft toss. That isn't going easy on them. It is setting the level where they can actually learn.

Try this at practice

  1. Run three groups, not one line. Hitting, fielding, and a game station. Rotate every 8 to 10 minutes.
  2. End with a game. Save the last 8 to 10 minutes for a game with almost no coaching. Pickle, a wiffle ball game, or a relay around the bases. Send them to the car happy.
  3. Adjust one drill on the spot. Watch for a drill where most kids are failing. Move closer, slow it down, or use a bigger target until most are succeeding.
  4. One specific note per player. Before they leave, tell each kid one thing you saw them do well. “I saw you run hard to back up that throw to first.” Specific beats “good job.”
  5. Correct up close. For one practice, give every correction face to face and quietly. Notice how it goes.

The bottom line

Your job in rec ball is not to build professional players. It is to give kids a reason to come back next year. A kid who stays gets years more practice than a kid who quits. Fun is not the enemy of development. It is what keeps kids around long enough to develop.

Try this with your kid

Play catch and find the distance where your kid catches about 7 of 10. If most throws miss, move closer. If nothing ever misses, back up a step. On the walk in, tell them one specific thing you saw them do well.

Guidelines & sources

  • Aspen Institute Project Play & Utah State University (2019). Survey of about 1,000 sports parents: average child plays under three years, quits around age 11; “not fun anymore” was the top reason. Parent report, not direct observation.
  • Visek, A. J., et al. (2015). The Fun Integration Theory (FUN MAPS). 81 fun determinants in 11 factors; being a good sport, trying hard, and positive coaching rated highest; winning ranked 48th of 81, gear last. Youth soccer, one region.
  • Crane, J., & Temple, V. (2015). Systematic review of 43 studies on dropout from organized youth sport. Lack of enjoyment, low perceived competence, social pressure, and competing priorities were common reasons.
  • Barnett, N. P., Smoll, F. L., & Smith, R. E. (1992). Coach Effectiveness Training and attrition in Little League. 5% vs. 26% dropout. Small sample (18 coaches).
  • Guadagnoli, M. A., & Lee, T. D. (2004). Challenge point framework: learning is best when difficulty fits the learner's skill. Does not specify a 70% target.

Note on certainty: The link between fun, feeling competent, and staying in sport is consistent across many studies, though most rely on surveys. The widely quoted “70% quit by 13” figure has no published data behind it, so we left it out. The 7-in-10 success target is a rule of thumb.
