Ask, Don't Tell: Coaching With Questions

The player was there. We were watching.

5 min read

A kid takes strike three, looking. She walks back to the dugout with her head down. Before she sits, we already have the fix loaded: “With two strikes, you have to protect the plate.”

It is true. It is also our answer to a moment she lived and we only watched. Try a different first sentence: “What did you see on that one?”

Maybe she says it looked outside. Maybe she says she froze. Either way, she just told us where her head was, and she had to go back and look at the pitch herself to say it. That is the whole idea of this Field Note. Ask first. Tell second, and only if she missed it.

The oldest coaching trick there is

In Plato's Meno, Socrates walks a boy through a geometry problem without telling him a single answer. He only asks questions. The boy gets it wrong, then finds his own mistake, then lands on the answer. Socrates' point was that the boy did the figuring, not the teacher.

That is a 2,400-year-old story, not a study. But it is a fair picture of what we are after. The player had the eyes, the hands, and the feel of the rep. We had a view from the dugout. A good question sends her back to the best data on the field: her own.

What the research says, and does not

Here is the honest version. The idea is promising. The proof is thin.

One season, one sport. Kristine Chambers and Joan Vickers followed competitive age-group swimmers through a season. One group's coach cut back feedback, saved it for bigger misses, and replaced much of it with questions. That group improved its technique more than the comparison group, and its race times improved more after the program ended. The swimmers also said they were doing more thinking and liked the back-and-forth with their coach. It is one small study in swimming. It points the right way. It does not prove anything for a 9U softball team.

Questioning is one of the tools in Vickers' Decision Training approach. For using it on live fielding reads, runners on, outs called, see How to Train Decision-Making, Not Just Mechanics. This page is the how-to for the questions themselves.

Asking is harder than it sounds. Researchers recorded five academy youth soccer coaches and studied how their questions actually played out (Cope and colleagues, 2016). Three patterns kept showing up. Coaches wanted an answer right away. Many questions were leading, with one right answer the coach already had in mind. And the coach still did most of the talking. These were full-time professionals. If it is hard for them, it will be hard for us. That is not a knock on anyone. It is just how talking works when we already know the answer.

A question is not magic. A 2021 chapter by Mark Partington makes the case that questioning gets oversold. Most coaching questions only ask a player to recall a fact, which keeps the coach in charge. His point: the type of question, and when we ask it, decides whether anyone learns.

Why it may help motivation too. A widely cited model of coach and athlete motivation lists asking for the athlete's input, and giving them chances to take initiative, among the coach behaviors that support a player's sense of ownership (Mageau and Vallerand, 2003). That is a model built from research, not a test of questioning itself. But it fits what we want: kids who feel the game belongs to them.

In plain English: Asking instead of telling has a small study behind it and a sensible reason behind it. Most of the benefit probably comes from asking the right kind of question, then actually waiting for the answer.

Three questions that are really answers

These sound like questions. They work like instructions.

Guess what I am thinking
“Should you have gone to second there?”

There is one right answer and the kid knows it is “yes.” She learns to read us, not the play.

Hurry up and answer
“Why did you do that? … Anybody? … Because the runner was forced.”

We asked, then filled the silence ourselves. Next time, nobody bothers to think.

The lecture with a question mark
“Don't you think you should keep your eye on it?”

Same message as telling. Just longer.

The question ladder

Open questions send the player back to the rep. Climb only as high as you need to.

1
“What did you see?”
Pulls attention back to the ball, the runner, the gap.
2
“What did you try?”
Gets her plan out loud. Now we know if it was a choice or a miss.
3
“What happened?”
She connects the plan to the result. No grading from us.
4
“What will you try next time?”
She owns the fix. If it is a good one, we just say “Go try it.”

If she nails it on step one, stop climbing. If she is stuck after step four, now we tell. One cue, aimed at the ball or the target.

Wait longer than feels normal

Classroom research by Mary Budd Rowe found that when teachers paused about three seconds after a question, instead of jumping in, students gave longer and more thoughtful answers. That was school, not a ball field. But three seconds of quiet is free. Count it in your head. It will feel like a long time. Let it.

Size it to the age

Ages 4-6
“Show me.”
Talking about a rep is hard at this age. Let them answer with their body. “Show me where the ball went.”
Ages 7-12
One open question.
“What did you see?” If you get “I don't know,” offer two choices: “Was it high or low?”
Ages 13-15
Why, then what if.
“Why there?” then “What if the runner was on second instead?”
Ages 16+
Let them run it.
Ask a player to ask the questions. Teaching it is the last step of owning it.

These bands are our coaching judgment, not research cutoffs. Know your kid.

When to just tell

  • Safety. Helmet off in the on-deck circle is not a discussion.
  • A brand-new skill. A kid who has never seen a rundown needs to see one before we ask her about it.
  • An upset kid. A crying player does not need a quiz. She needs a minute. See A Player Is Crying.
  • Between pitches in a game. Save the questions for the dugout, the car ride, or Tuesday.

Try this at practice

  • Ask first. After any rep, the first thing out of our mouth is a question. Telling is allowed, just second.
  • Freeze. In a live game at practice, yell “Freeze!” Everybody stops where they are. Ask one player, “Where should you be right now?” Then play on.
  • Count your words. Have a parent tally questions versus instructions for one practice. No judgment, just a number. In one study, youth soccer coaches who reviewed video of their own sessions over three seasons cut back on instruction and most of them asked more questions (Partington and colleagues, 2015).
  • One question to close. End practice with “What is one thing you figured out today?” Let three kids answer. Do not add to it.
The whole thing in one line
Ask what they saw. Wait. Then add only what they missed.

Guidelines & sources

  • Plato. Meno, the geometry passage. Philosophy, used here as a picture of the method, not as evidence.
  • Chambers, K. L., & Vickers, J. N. (2006). Effects of bandwidth feedback and questioning on the performance of competitive swimmers. The Sport Psychologist, 20(2). One season, age-group swimmers, small sample.
  • Cope, E., Partington, M., Cushion, C. J., & Harvey, S. (2016). An investigation of professional top-level youth football coaches' questioning practice. Qualitative Research in Sport, Exercise and Health. Five academy coaches; describes how questioning plays out, does not test outcomes.
  • Partington, M. (2021). “Questioning in coaching leads to learning”: a deconstruction of questioning. In Myths of Sport Coaching. Argues questions only help when type and timing are chosen with care.
  • Partington, M., Cushion, C. J., Cope, E., & Harvey, S. (2015). The impact of video feedback on professional youth football coaches' reflection and practice behaviour. Reflective Practice, 16(5). Five coaches over three seasons.
  • Mageau, G. A., & Vallerand, R. J. (2003). The coach-athlete relationship: a motivational model. Journal of Sports Sciences. A model drawn from prior research, not a test of questioning.
  • Harvey, S., & Light, R. L. (2015). Questioning for learning in game-based approaches to teaching and coaching. Asia-Pacific Journal of Health, Sport and Physical Education. Practical questioning techniques, including freezing play.
  • Rowe, M. B. (1986). Wait time: slowing down may be a way of speeding up. Journal of Teacher Education, 37(1). Classroom research, not sport.

Note on certainty: the only outcome study here is one small swimming study. The rest describes how coaches question, argues about it, or comes from classrooms. No study we found tests questioning with youth baseball or softball teams. We hold this as a low-risk habit worth trying, not a proven method.
