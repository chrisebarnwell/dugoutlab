The Development Playbook

How to build a baseball or softball athlete, the right way, at every age.

A plain-language, research-backed guide to physical development from first-ball through the pros. Built on what the science actually says, not on what is trending. This is the thinking behind every DugoutLab training resource.

The 30-second version

Train the athlete before the sport. Between roughly ages 7 and 13, the best thing you can do for a young player is build a strong, coordinated, multi-sport mover. Bodyweight strength, jumping, sprinting, throwing, catching, rotating, and playing many sports. Heavy weights, max-effort lifts, and bodybuilding splits do not belong in this window. What does: high-quality movement, light rotational work, and an adult who watches how the kid moves.

The one rule that governs everything

Readiness beats birthday. Two kids the same age can be two years apart in physical maturity. Train the body in front of you, not the number on the form. Form earns the load, every time.

Three rules that govern every age

These do not change from age 6 to the pros. Only the dose changes.

01

Readiness over age

Maturity, not a birthday, sets the program. Progress on what the athlete can control with clean form. Earlier is not better. Ready is better.

02

Fun is the engine

In surveys of why kids quit sports, losing the fun is consistently the top answer. Bored kids quit. Variety and play drive development as much as any drill.

03

Move well, then load

Master bodyweight patterns before adding any external weight. Squat, hinge, push, pull, brace, land, carry. Quality reps beat heavy reps.

The three things to protect

Development is mostly about not breaking the athlete. These three matter more than any drill.

The growth spurt

During the fastest growth phase (Peak Height Velocity), bones grow faster than muscles and tendons. Many kids go through a clumsy patch, and overuse complaints at the knee (Osgood-Schlatter) and heel (Sever's) become more common in this window. Signs: a sudden height jump, recent shoe-size change, new complaints of knee or heel pain. The right response is to lower the jumping and high-impact volume, lean into mobility and technique, and never push through joint pain. Heavier strength work waits until after the spurt, when tissue has caught up and form can lead the load.

Single-sport specialization

Highly specialized young athletes show higher rates of overuse injury in clinical studies (Jayanthi and colleagues), and a 2022 meta-analysis (Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick) found that world-class adult athletes typically played more sports and specialized later than near-elite peers. Specialization is one associated risk factor among several, not a proven single cause of burnout. But the direction of the evidence supports sampling through roughly age 13 and delaying year-round single-sport play.

Energy and recovery (especially for girls)

Female athletes mature earlier and carry a much higher ACL injury risk, and are more vulnerable to under-fueling during growth (known as RED-S). Neuromuscular warm-up programs that teach soft, aligned landings (PEP, FIFA 11+) reduce ACL injury rates by roughly half in meta-analyses and should be standard from late childhood on. Enough food, enough sleep, and managed load protect both performance and long-term health.

A note on throwing

This playbook is about athletic development: strength, power, speed, movement. It does not cover throwing or pitching programs. Throwing volume is the highest-risk variable for a young arm and has to be coordinated with the athlete's actual team and game schedule by someone qualified, watching that specific athlete. We keep it separate on purpose.

What this is built on

Every recommendation here traces back to recognized position statements and peer-reviewed research. The full reference list:

Guidelines and sources this playbook is based on

  • NSCA Youth Resistance Training Position Statement. Faigenbaum, Kraemer, Blimkie, Jeffreys, Micheli, Nitka & Rowland. Journal of Strength & Conditioning Research, 2009.
  • NSCA Position Statement on Long-Term Athletic Development. Lloyd, Cronin, Faigenbaum, Haff, Howard, Kraemer, Micheli, Myer & Oliver, 2016.
  • The Youth Physical Development Model. Lloyd & Oliver. Strength & Conditioning Journal, 2012.
  • Position Statement on Youth Resistance Training: the 2014 International Consensus. Lloyd et al. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2014.
  • USOPC American Development Model (ADM) — multi-sport participation and readiness-over-age principles.
  • Szymanski et al. — medicine-ball and rotational training research in baseball populations.
  • Mandelbaum et al. (PEP program) and the FIFA 11+ — neuromuscular ACL-injury-prevention evidence in female athletes.
  • Canadian Sport for Life LTAD framework (Balyi et al.) — source of the stage names. Used here as a planning framework only; its original "windows of trainability" claims are contested and we do not rely on them.
  • Jayanthi et al. — sports specialization and overuse injury risk in young athletes (multiple clinical studies).
  • Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick (2022). Meta-analysis of early specialization vs. sampling and adult performance. Perspectives on Psychological Science.
  • Brenner / AAP Clinical Report (2016) — sports specialization and intensive training in young athletes. Pediatrics.
  • Mountjoy et al. (IOC consensus, 2014/2018) — Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S).
  • Peak Height Velocity / maturation literature — growth-related conditions (Osgood-Schlatter, Sever's) and injury-risk patterns around the growth spurt.

Educational resource only. Not medical advice. This page explains general principles of youth athletic development. It is not a personalized training prescription and does not replace a qualified strength coach, athletic trainer, or physician who can see your athlete. Before starting any program, especially around a growth spurt or any pain, check with a professional. Some evidence here is strong and some is emerging; we have tried to be honest about the difference.
