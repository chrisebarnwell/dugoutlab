The Development Playbook

How to build a baseball or softball athlete, the right way, at every age.

A plain-language guide to physical development from first-ball through the pros. Built on youth-training position statements and research, and honest about where the evidence is strong and where it is thin. This is the thinking behind every DugoutLab training resource.

Baseball & Softball Ages 6 to Pro Bodyweight First Sources Shown

The 30-second version

Train the athlete before the sport. Between roughly ages 7 and 13, focus on building a strong, coordinated, multi-sport mover. Bodyweight strength, jumping, sprinting, throwing, catching, rotating, and playing many sports. The major youth guidelines (NSCA 2009; International Consensus 2014) say resistance training is safe and effective for kids when it is well designed and supervised by a qualified adult. Most volunteer teams do not have that coach, so our rule is bodyweight first, no loaded dynamic lifts before puberty, and no max-effort lifting. What fits: high-quality movement, light rotational work, and an adult who watches how the kid moves.

The one rule that governs everything

Readiness beats birthday. Two kids the same age can be two years apart in physical maturity. Train the body in front of you, not the number on the form. Form earns the load, every time.

Three rules that govern every age

These do not change from age 6 to the pros. Only the dose changes.

01

Readiness over age

Maturity, not a birthday, sets the program. Progress on what the athlete can control with clean form. Earlier is not better. Ready is better.

02

Fun is the engine

In a national survey of kids who quit a sport, "not having fun" was the top reason (Aspen Institute & Utah State, 2019). Variety and play keep them coming back.

03

Move well, then load

Master bodyweight patterns before adding external weight. Squat, hinge, push, pull, brace, land, carry. Clean reps first, load later.

The development map

Five stages from first-ball to the pros. The stage names come from the Canadian Sport for Life LTAD framework (Balyi), a coaching consensus model, not tested science. Ages are guides, not gates, and they shift about a year or two earlier for girls. Tap any stage to see what to build and what to avoid.

The three things to protect

A big part of development is keeping the athlete healthy. These three deserve your attention.

The growth spurt

During the fastest growth phase (Peak Height Velocity), bones can outpace muscles and tendons for a while. Many kids go through a clumsy patch, and overuse complaints at the knee (Osgood-Schlatter) and heel (Sever's) become more common in this window. Signs: a sudden height jump, recent shoe-size change, new complaints of knee or heel pain. Our response: lower the jumping and high-impact (plyometric) volume, lean into mobility and technique, and never push through joint pain. Our conservative rule is that heavier strength work waits until after the spurt. The position statements allow supervised strength training through puberty; we hold back because most volunteer teams lack a qualified strength coach.

Single-sport specialization

Highly specialized young athletes show higher rates of overuse injury in clinical studies (Jayanthi and colleagues, mostly case-control and retrospective designs), and a 2022 meta-analysis (Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick) found that world-class adult athletes typically played more sports and specialized later than near-elite peers. Specialization is one associated risk factor among several, not a proven single cause of burnout. Still, the direction of the evidence, and groups like the AAP (Brenner, 2016) and AOSSM, support sampling through the early teens and delaying year-round single-sport play.

Energy and recovery (especially for girls)

Female athletes mature earlier and carry a higher ACL injury risk than males in the same sports. Any athlete can be under-fueled during growth (known as RED-S). Neuromuscular warm-up programs that teach soft, aligned landings (PEP, FIFA 11+) cut ACL injury rates by roughly half in meta-analyses (Webster & Hewett, 2018), mostly in soccer, basketball, and handball. We think they are worth making standard from late childhood on. Enough food, enough sleep, and managed load protect both performance and long-term health.

A note on throwing

This playbook is about athletic development: strength, power, speed, movement. It does not cover throwing or pitching programs. Throwing volume is the highest-risk variable for a young arm and has to be coordinated with the athlete's actual team and game schedule by someone qualified, watching that specific athlete. We keep it separate on purpose.

What this is built on

Most recommendations here trace to recognized position statements and peer-reviewed research. Where a rule is our own conservative choice, we say so. The full reference list:

Guidelines and sources this playbook is based on

  • NSCA Youth Resistance Training Position Statement. Faigenbaum, Kraemer, Blimkie, Jeffreys, Micheli, Nitka & Rowland. Journal of Strength & Conditioning Research, 2009.
  • NSCA Position Statement on Long-Term Athletic Development. Lloyd, Cronin, Faigenbaum, Haff, Howard, Kraemer, Micheli, Myer & Oliver, 2016.
  • The Youth Physical Development Model. Lloyd & Oliver. Strength & Conditioning Journal, 2012.
  • Position Statement on Youth Resistance Training: the 2014 International Consensus. Lloyd et al. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2014.
  • USOPC American Development Model (ADM) and USA Baseball Long-Term Athlete Development Plan: multi-sport participation and readiness-over-age principles. Consensus frameworks.
  • Szymanski et al.: medicine-ball and rotational training research in baseball players, including high school players (JSCR, 2007).
  • Mandelbaum et al. (PEP program) and the FIFA 11+: neuromuscular ACL-injury-prevention evidence in female athletes. Webster & Hewett (2018), Journal of Orthopaedic Research: meta-analysis of meta-analyses of ACL injury reduction programs.
  • Canadian Sport for Life LTAD framework (Balyi et al.): source of the stage names. A consensus framework, not tested science. Used here as a planning framework only; its "windows of trainability" claims are contested and we do not rely on them.
  • Ford, De Ste Croix, Lloyd et al. (2011). The Long-Term Athlete Development model: physiological evidence and application. Journal of Sports Sciences. Critique of the trainability-window claims.
  • Aspen Institute Project Play & Utah State University (2019). National youth sports survey: "not having fun" as the top reason kids quit.
  • Jayanthi et al.: sports specialization and overuse injury risk in young athletes (multiple clinical studies).
  • Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick (2022). Meta-analysis of early specialization vs. sampling and adult performance. Perspectives on Psychological Science.
  • Brenner / AAP Clinical Report (2016): sports specialization and intensive training in young athletes. Pediatrics. Also the AOSSM Early Sport Specialization consensus (LaPrade et al., 2016).
  • Mountjoy et al. (IOC consensus, 2014/2018): Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S).
  • Peak Height Velocity / maturation literature: growth-related conditions (Osgood-Schlatter, Sever's) and injury-risk patterns around the growth spurt.

Educational resource only. Not medical advice. This page explains general principles of youth athletic development. It is not a personalized training prescription and does not replace a qualified strength coach, athletic trainer, or physician who can see your athlete. Before starting any program, especially around a growth spurt or any pain, check with a professional. Some evidence here is strong, some is emerging, and some rules are our own conservative calls. We have tried to be honest about the difference.
