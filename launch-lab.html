    The science inside

    Three forces act on the ball. Gravity pulls it down. Drag pushes against its motion and grows with the square of speed, so a ball hit twice as hard feels about four times the drag. Backspin creates lift (the Magnus force), which is why a well-hit fly ball hangs and carries.

    No air, no drag. After each swing we show how far the same ball would go in a vacuum. The gap between the two numbers is what the air did.

    Thin air carries. At a mile high the air is roughly a fifth less dense, so there is less drag and less lift, and the drag drop wins. Same swing, more distance.

    Fielders are a race against hang time. A fielder reacts, then runs. If he can cover the distance before the ball lands, it is an out. On ground balls the race is fielder plus throw against the runner going to first.

    The sweet spot. Statcast calls launch angles from 8 to 32 degrees the sweet spot. Below 10 is a ground ball, 10 to 25 a line drive, 25 to 50 a fly ball, above 50 a pop-up.

    For coaches

    This is a thinking toy, not a swing lesson. Kids must call the result before every swing. That is on purpose: guessing first, then seeing why the guess was wrong, is where the learning happens. The scientist score rewards good calls and changing one thing at a time, not big hits.

    Best use: sit next to them. When a call is wrong, ask one question: what surprised you?

    Guidelines & sources this game follows

    • Nathan, A. M. The Physics of Baseball (University of Illinois). Drag, lift, and how air density changes carry. baseball.physics.illinois.edu
    • Sawicki, G. S., Hubbard, M., and Stronge, W. J. (2003). How to hit home runs. American Journal of Physics. The lift model used here.
    • MLB Statcast glossary. Exit velocity, launch angle, sweet spot (8 to 32 degrees), batted-ball types. mlb.com/glossary/statcast
    • Crouch, C., Fagen, A. P., Callan, J. P., and Mazur, E. (2004). Classroom demonstrations: Learning tools or entertainment? American Journal of Physics, 72(6). Students who predicted the outcome before a demonstration understood it better than students who only watched. The reason this game makes you call it first.
    • Lewis, M. (2003). Moneyball. Measure what happens instead of trusting how it looks.
    • Lindbergh, B., and Sawchik, T. (2019). The MVP Machine. How measurement like exit velocity and launch angle changed player development.
