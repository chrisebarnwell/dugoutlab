How Kids Learn a Moving Game

10 min read · The thinking behind the plans on DugoutLab

Baseball is not a knowledge test. It is a moving problem. A ball comes off a bat at an angle you have never seen, on a hop you cannot predict, and your body has about one second to produce an answer. Coaching kids well means coaching for that reality.

Our rule is simple: truth over philosophy. Evidence decides, not the lens. Most of what we use comes from skill acquisition, the research on how people learn to move. Some of it is solid. Some of it is a useful way of thinking that has not been proven better than the alternatives. We tell you which is which.

Our default is games first, drills where they help. Drill to get it started. Play to make it work in a game. Here are the five ideas that run through the site, and how sure we are about each.

1. Performance is not learning

A kid who fields twenty clean grounders in a row looks like he is learning. Some of that is just performing. The classic lab study is Shea and Morgan (1979): adults who practiced a task in a fixed order looked better in practice, while adults who practiced in a mixed order looked worse in practice and did better on a later test. Researchers call this contextual interference. Robert Bjork calls the broader idea desirable difficulties, mostly from verbal and lab learning.

Here is the honest part. A 2024 meta-analysis (Czyz and colleagues) found the mixed-practice benefit close to zero in applied settings, and not significant for young players. One study of college hitters (Hall, Domingues and Cavazos, 1994) did favor mixed pitches. And the challenge point framework (Guadagnoli and Lee, 2004) says beginners need simpler, more repetitive practice first.

The coach's translation: today's practice is not the final grade. Straight reps help a brand-new skill get started. Once a kid can do it, mix it up so it looks more like a game. Neither choice is a disaster. Read more: When to Mix Up Your Practice Reps.

2. Perception and action are one skill

A fielder does not see the ball and then decide to move. The seeing and the moving are coupled. This idea comes from ecological psychology (James Gibson) and is the core of the ecological dynamics framework developed by researchers like Keith Davids and popularized for coaches by Rob Gray. The skill of hitting is not only "a swing." It is reading a pitch and swinging. That is a strong idea and a useful lens. It is not proven superior to other coaching methods for kids.

So we lean on live reads, front toss at mixed speeds, and games, and we still use focused mechanics work when a kid needs it. The best comparison we have (Bromilow and colleagues, 2025, 9 studies, mostly soccer, small samples) found game-based and drill-based training mostly tied on technique, with a modest edge for game-based on decision making. No youth baseball studies were in it.

3. The environment is your best assistant coach

The constraints-led approach says you can often change a player's movement by changing the task or environment instead of giving another instruction. Shrink the field, change the ball, add a rule, create a consequence. The setup does some of the teaching so you can talk less. We treat it as a guiding lens, not a proven winner. Read the full article: Let the Game Teach the Game.

Short coach talk and an external focus (cue the ball or the target, not the body) fit here too. External focus is cheap and worth trying. Wulf's 2013 review supports it, but a 2024 reanalysis (McKay and colleagues) found the average effect small to nil after correcting for publication bias.

4. Small-sided games multiply everything

Three kids playing a small game touch the ball more and make more decisions than twelve kids standing in one line. That part is just counting. Most of the research on small-sided games comes from soccer and other invasion sports, so we do not claim it has been proven in baseball. USA Baseball's American Development Model recommends modified, small-sided play. Small groups are the point, not games versus drills: a drill with three kids and no line also keeps reps high.

5. Fun is the core value. Make them want to come back tomorrow.

This is the idea we hold hardest, and the evidence for it is the most direct. Amanda Visek's FUN MAPS study (2015, youth soccer players) asked kids what makes sport fun. The top factors were being a good sport, trying hard, and positive coaching. Winning ranked 48th of 81. In an Aspen Institute and Utah State survey (2019), "not having fun" was the top reason kids gave for quitting, and the average kid quit around age 11. In Little League, kids whose coaches got a short training on positive coaching dropped out at 5%, versus 26% for untrained coaches (Barnett, Smoll and Smith, 1992).

A kid who wants to come back gets another season of reps. That is the simplest case for fun we know.

When to introduce what

The ideas above apply at every age. The mix changes. Here is our rough map, matched to the 4 Stages framework. It is our planning judgment, not a tested prescription:

Ages (approx.)What we emphasize
4 to 7Play, movement variety, and games with simple rules. Very little technical talk. Tag games, scatter ball, free swings, plus quick simple drills to start a new skill. The goal is loving the ball and the field.
7 to 9Small-sided games become the backbone. Short drills start new skills, then games put them to work. Introduce one-cue coaching and simple constraints. Tag-style novelty games still land well here.
10 to 12Keep the games, raise the stakes. More mixed practice once skills are in, live reads, situational play. Many kids this age want real competition more than themed games. Drop the theme, keep the constraint.
13 and upGame-realistic reps carry most of the load. Focused technical work has a clear place for specific fixes, then gets folded back into live play. Players start owning more of their own training.
One honest caution: ages are approximations. Maturity varies by years within one birthday. Coach the kid in front of you, not the calendar. And the biggest debate in this space, repetition versus play, has its own page: Drillers Make Killers vs. Let Them Play.
