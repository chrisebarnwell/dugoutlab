The three rules that govern every age

  • Readiness over age. Maturity, not a birthday, sets the program. Two kids the same age can be two years apart. Train the body in front of you.
  • Fun is the engine. In surveys of why kids quit sports, losing the fun is consistently the top answer. Reward effort and showing up. Bored kids quit.
  • Move well, then load. Master bodyweight patterns before any external weight. Quality reps beat heavy reps every time.

Guidelines & sources this section follows

  • NSCA Position Statement on Long-Term Athletic Development (Lloyd et al., 2016). Resistance training is safe and beneficial for youth when supervised and technique-led.
  • NSCA Position Statement on Youth Resistance Training (Faigenbaum et al., 2009). Bodyweight mastery and movement quality before external load.
  • AAP Clinical Report: Sports Specialization and Intensive Training (Brenner, 2016). Workload, rest, and multi-sport guidance.
  • IOC Consensus Statement on Youth Athletic Development (Bergeron et al., 2015). Growth, maturation, and injury risk around the growth spurt.
  • Pitch Smart / ASMI guidelines. Throwing volume and rest for baseball arms.

A caution we take seriously: the popular "windows of trainability" idea from early LTAD writing is contested in the research. We treat stages as a planning framework, not a biological law. Anything on these pages without a source behind it gets cut.

Educational resource only. Not medical advice. These pages explain general principles of youth athletic development. They are not a personalized training prescription and do not replace a qualified strength coach, athletic trainer, or physician who can see your athlete. Around a growth spurt or with any pain, check with a professional first.
