The Hit Window

In a cage, every ball hits a net. Nobody can tell a line drive to the gap from a routine fly ball. This tool works backward from your field to show which launch angles land in open grass. Then it tells you where to hang a target so the cage shows it.

      How this was built
      We worked backward from the field.
      1. Launch a ball at every angle from 10 degrees down to 60 degrees up, at the exit velocity you set.
      2. Fly it through the air with gravity and drag until it lands.
      3. Ask one question about each ball. Does it land where a fielder can get to it?
      4. The angles that clear the infield and land before an outfielder arrives are the hit window.
      5. Trace those same angles from the tee to your net. That is where the target hangs.

      Guidelines & sources this tool follows

      • MLB Statcast glossary, Launch Angle. Ground ball below 10 degrees, line drive 10 to 25, fly ball 25 to 50, pop up above 50. Measured on major league hitters. Used here as a cross-check, not as the youth answer.
      • Kagan, D., and Nathan, A. M. (2014). Simplified models for the drag coefficient of a pitched baseball. The Physics Teacher. Drag coefficient of roughly 0.31 to 0.35 from pitch tracking. We use 0.35 for every ball and speed. That is a simplification.
      • Nathan, A. M. Trajectory Calculator, University of Illinois. The full model includes lift from backspin. Ours leaves spin out, so real fly balls carry farther than shown, most of all on hard-hit balls.
      • FanGraphs Community, Evaluating Statcast Hit-Type Boundaries. In MLB data, balls at 20 to 25 degrees act like fly balls unless they are hit softly. Blog analysis, not peer reviewed.
      • Exit velocity presets. Rough ranges published by coaching and training sites. We found no peer-reviewed youth dataset. Blast Motion states that no trustworthy softball chart by age exists. Flagged uncertain. Measure your own hitters.
      • Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., and Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin. Across 128 experiments, tangible rewards tied to performance lowered free-choice motivation, and the effect was stronger in children. Praise did not. The coin in this game is a performance reward, even when the only opponent is your own last round. Youth fit in a cage game is untested. Flagged uncertain. Watch whether kids still want to hit when no coin is on the line.
      • Phone sight. Reads the tilt sensor in your phone. Sensor accuracy varies by phone. Check one spot against the table and a tape measure before you trust it. On a field the phone gives the direction only. Tilt is too rough to judge distance past the infield, so distance is paced off.
      • Fielder depth, speed, reach, and reaction time. DugoutLab assumptions. Not measured. Open the field setup and change them.

      Note on certainty: this is a physics model, not a record of real youth games. It treats every ball as hit straight at a fielder, so it misses the gaps, and it ignores spin, wind, bad hops, and errors. In youth games a ground ball often becomes a baserunner. The model does not count that. We hold the window as a starting point for a target, not a rule for a swing.
