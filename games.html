The Games Library

These aren't textbook drills. These are games we actually ran with our 8 and 9-year-olds. We lean on them for a simple reason: small-sided, game-like play gives kids more touches, more decisions, and more fun than standing in line. Does it beat drills for learning? The research (mostly small soccer studies) finds about a draw on technique and a modest edge for games on decision-making. So we play first and drill when a brand-new skill needs it. The specific games are ours. Most of these land hardest at ages 6 to 10; older kids want the competition without the costume, so strip the theme and keep the constraint.

Source: Bromilow et al. (2025), Sports Medicine Open. What the games vs drills research says →

Game 01 · The Opener

Dodgeball Warmup

WARMUP 10–15 MIN ANY AGE
What It Looks Like

Two teams. Soft rubber dodgeballs (or rolled-up socks if you forgot the balls). Split the field down the middle with cones. Standard dodgeball rules. If you get hit, you sit. If you catch one, the thrower sits. Play until one team is out, then reset.

Setup · What You Need
  • 4–6 soft balls (no hardballs. This is warmup, not war.)
  • One rule: hits below the shoulders only
  • Cones to mark a center line
  • 10x30 area, roughly
Why we start here: Kids show up tight. Parents dropped them off, they're worried about what they're going to do well or poorly. Dodgeball usually shakes most of it loose. By the time you call them in, they're laughing, breathing hard, and ready to actually listen. You're not warming up arms yet. You're warming up the kid.
Game 02 · Throwing

Back-Up Catch

THROWING 10 MIN AGES 7+
What It Looks Like

Pair up the team. Start with partners 15 feet apart, normal catch. Every clean exchange both partners take a step back. Miss it, partners stay where they are. Drop it, take a step in. The team that ends up farthest apart wins.

Repeat 2–3 times. Set a max-distance cone line that fits the age, so it stays a catch game and never turns into a long-throw contest.

The Constraint That Makes It Work

Partners can only step back if the catch was clean. No bobbles. No on-a-hop. No reaching out and dropping it. This forces both throwers to throw accurately and catchers to actually move their feet.

Why we like it better than plain catch: A regular catch warmup has no consequence, and kids zone out. Put "if you catch this, you get to back up" on it and every throw and every catch matters. It won't teach a throwing motion by itself. It gives kids a reason to care about every rep.
Game 03 · The One We Run Every Practice

2-2 Relay Race

FIELDING THROWING 15–20 MIN AGES 7+
What It Looks Like

Split the team into pairs. Pairs line up at home plate (or the foul line). Coach rolls or hits a ball to the outfield fence. On the call, the first pair sprints out together. One player goes all the way to the ball. The other player stops at the cutoff position and waits.

While the outfielder runs down the ball, the cutoff gets lined up between the ball and the target base, arms up, yelling "Hit me!" The outfielder hits the cutoff. The cutoff catches it turning to the glove side, listens for the call from a coach or teammate at the target ("Home!" or "Two!"), and relays it there.

Time them. Run 2–3 rounds. Switch which kid is the outfielder, which is the cutoff.

Why We Run It Every Practice

At the 8 and 9-year-old level, the ball gets out to the outfield and dies there. The outfielder picks it up, throws it 20 feet, and now there's no play. The runners take an extra base. The defense never has a chance.

Getting the ball to a cutoff fixes a lot of that. It is one of the biggest gaps we see at this age, and it is a hard thing to learn from a diagram.

Make it a race and they want to run it again.

The constraint that does the teaching: The outfielder can't throw home directly. They have to hit the cutoff. The cutoff has to be lined up before the throw comes. Kids start finding the straight line between ball and base on their own, and you fill in the rest.
Game 04 · Fielding

Tennis Ball Fly Game

FIELDING 15 MIN AGES 7+
What It Looks Like

Two teams. No gloves. Coach hits tennis balls into the air (a tennis racket works great for this). The fielding team has to catch the fly balls bare-handed.

Every ball that drops is a run for the other team. One catch is 3 outs: instant inning over. Teams flip sides. Play a few innings. Fewest runs given up wins.

Why No Gloves

A tennis ball is soft enough to catch barehanded, and bare hands give fast feedback. Kids who try to catch it with stiff hands or one hand find out quickly that it pops out. Soft hands, two hands, eyes on it. The ball gives the feedback so you don't have to.

The hidden lesson: "Catch = inning over" forces every kid to actually try. If you've ever watched an outfielder drift off during a long inning, this gives them a reason to lock in. They sprint to every ball because catching it ends the inning for their team.
Game 05 · Pre-Game Ritual

Hat Tag

WARMUP 8–10 MIN ANY AGE
What It Looks Like

Every kid tucks their hat into the back of their belt or waistband, like a flag. Free-for-all in a defined area. Kids try to grab other players' hats while protecting their own. Lose your hat, you're out and you sit. Last kid with a hat wins.

5 minutes a round. Run 2–3 rounds.

Why We Use It as a Warmup

What it gets them doing:

  • Athleticism: cutting, change of direction, body control
  • Spatial awareness: head on a swivel, reading the field
  • Competitiveness: with stakes that don't matter

It almost always gets a laugh. Kids who showed up tired are usually ready by the end of round two.

Why it's the perfect pre-game ritual: Kids run onto the field already loose, already moving, already connected to each other. No "circle up and stretch." They're already a team by first pitch.
Game 06 · Cross-Training

Small-Sided Football Catch

THROWING 10–15 MIN AGES 7+
What It Looks Like

Bring a Nerf football to practice. Split into teams of 3 or 4. Small field. Touchdown counts as a score. You can't run with the ball. You have to throw to a teammate to advance. Defense plays one-hand touch.

Play in short bursts. 5-minute games. Switch teams. Keep moving.

Why a Football in Baseball Practice

It is still throwing, catching, and moving to open space, just with a different ball. Kids have to find a teammate, get open, and decide fast.

It also breaks up the routine. A fresh game is an easy way to get a flat group re-engaged.

Watch their faces: The kid who's been checked out during throwing drills often lights up when you put a football in his hand. An engaged kid gets more out of whatever comes next.
Game 07 · Hot Day Special

Water Balloon Fights

FUN 15–20 MIN SUMMER ONLY
What It Looks Like

Fill 50+ water balloons before practice (this is the heavy lift, literally). Bring them in coolers. Split the team. Cones for a center line. Throw them at each other. That's the game.

For a slight skill twist: balloons can only be thrown overhand, from a proper throwing motion. Sidearm-tosses or shovel-throws don't count.

Honest Notes
  • Filling balloons takes 30–45 minutes. Bring help.
  • Bring a trash bag. The field will be covered in latex pieces. Clean every one up. Animals eat them.
  • Tell parents in advance. Kids get soaked.
  • Save this for the end of a hot practice. Reward, not opener.
Why it earns its place: Kids remember days like this long after the season. You can be a great coach and never do this. But if you want them to remember you, do it once. The signal you send: "We have fun here. The work matters, and so does the joy."
Game 08 · End-of-Season Party

Players vs. Old Farts

SEASON FINALE 60–90 MIN ALL AGES
What It Looks Like

Last day of the season. Kids vs. parents. Players bat normally. Parents have to bat with handicaps. Wiffle bats, softballs, no warm-up swings. They have to carry weights when they run the bases. They might have to do an obstacle course on the way to first.

The more ridiculous the handicaps, the better. Get creative. Make parents hop on one foot to second base. Make them spin around a bat before they run.

Kids play it straight. Try hard. Try to win. The parents do their best, but the deck is stacked.

Why This Is the Best Day of the Season

The kids absolutely lose their minds. Three months of being coached by these adults, and now they get to compete against them and win. Parents are sweaty and laughing. Grandparents are heckling from the bleachers. Younger siblings are getting in on it.

You will never get a better photo of a season than the moment your team mobs a parent at home plate.

Logistics That Matter
  • Tell parents in writing 3 weeks ahead. Some need convincing.
  • Recruit 9+ parents. If you only have 5, draft the older siblings.
  • Bring extra wiffle bats, softballs, soft baseballs, whatever you've got. Variety is the joke.
  • Pizza after. Trophy ceremony if you do one. Awards.
  • Take a team photo. Take it before everyone leaves.
The bigger picture: Every kid finishes the season with a memory of winning. Not winning a game that mattered. Winning a game where they got to mob their dad at home plate while their mom recorded it on her phone. That's what they tell their friends about. That's the kind of memory that makes signing up next year an easy call. Your one job is to make them want to come back tomorrow. This is how you cash that check.
